## Concede the voter file, then ask for the product

Political data has a real public-record core: name, address and party
registration may come from a state voter file the requester cannot claw back. A
letter that ignores that invites a reply scoped entirely to it — *"this is public
record"* — which is true and answers nothing.

So the letter says so first and offers the exemption as acceptable where it
applies. Everything it then asks for is what Aristotle **built on top** of the
public record, which is the actual product and is not itself a public record:

- **Modelled political attributes** — partisanship, ideology, turnout and
  vote-propensity scores, persuadability, issue affinity. No election office
  published these. They are inferences, they are consequential, and they are
  invisible to the subject.
- **Inferred religion, ethnicity or national origin**, asked for by name because
  a general assurance is least informative in exactly those categories.
- **Donation history matched by name-and-address** rather than by a disclosed
  identifier — matching is where the errors live, and a misattributed
  contribution is a false statement about someone's politics.
- **Consumer data appended to the voter record** — phones, emails, income
  estimates, household composition. Appending these produces something well
  beyond what the state published, and the appended fields are not public
  records at all.
- **Identifier edges**, not just rows.
- **Which campaign, committee or consultancy clients received it**, since those
  copies are what generate contact.

**The refresh question is the one to insist on.** The file is rebuilt from state
voter data on a cycle, so a deletion without a suppression entry that survives
the refresh is undone automatically. The letter asks for that confirmation
explicitly rather than accepting a general "removed".

**Multiple records are likely.** Several prior addresses sit in different
Pennsylvania counties, which routinely produces unreconciled duplicates in voter
files. The letter asks how many matched.

## If they ignore you

Work down this list. Each rung costs them more than the one above it.

1. **Reply in the existing thread** after the statutory deadline. California
   allows 45 days for a deletion request (Cal. Civ. Code 1798.130), extendable
   once by a further 45 with notice. Quote the date you first wrote.
2. **Write to the legal entity at the registered address above**, by post, if
   email has failed. A letter to the address of record is harder to lose than a
   support ticket, and it establishes a paper trail.
3. **Complain to the California Attorney General**, who administers the data
   broker registry: <https://oag.ca.gov/contact/consumer-complaint-against-business-or-company>.
   A broker's registration is what obliges it to answer; a complaint referencing
   the registry entry is the pressure point.
4. **Complain to the FTC**: <https://reportfraud.ftc.gov>. Useful for a pattern
   of non-response rather than a single case.
5. **Your own state Attorney General.** Many states with no comprehensive
   privacy statute still have consumer-protection powers and will take a
   complaint about a business that ignores its own published policy.

**What not to bother with:** phoning a support line to argue. The person who
answers cannot change the policy and did not write it. The registry entry, the
statutory deadline and the regulator are what actually move a company.
