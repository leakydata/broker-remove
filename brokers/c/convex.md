## "No account" is not "no record"

Convex answered a deletion request with:

> *"After a review of our systems, we are unable to provide you with any responsive
> information because we are unable to locate any record of an account or other
> business relationship with you."*

That is a negative to a question nobody asked. The request was about **personal
information held**, and the answer is about an **account or business relationship**
-- and for a data broker those come apart completely, because the ordinary case is
holding data about people who have no relationship with the company at all.

Convex sells commercial property and business intelligence: owners, occupants,
managers, decision-makers, and skip-traced or appended contact details attached to
those records. None of that requires the person in the record to be a customer. So
a search of the CRM is not a search of the product, and a clean CRM proves nothing
about the database being sold.

**How to answer it.** Do not argue about whether they searched properly -- ask them
to confirm a specific, checkable list, phrased so that a yes or a no both settle it:

  1. a property record naming me as owner, occupant, manager or decision-maker,
     at any prior address and not only the current one;
  2. any appended or skip-traced telephone number or email address attached to
     such a record;
  3. any inferred attribute, classification, score or segment derived from either.

Then say plainly that a negative on all three closes the matter with thanks. That
distinguishes the two cases that otherwise look identical -- a company that holds
nothing, and a company that searched the wrong system -- and it does so without
accusing them of the second.

## The retention sentence is fine

> *"We are required by law to maintain a record of your request and our response."*

That is legitimate and not worth fighting. A compliance log is the mechanism that
makes a suppression durable. The thing to ask for is only that it stay a log: that
it be limited to what the obligation requires and not become an active record in
any product.

This entry was briefly and wrongly downgraded from `not_found` to `submitted` on

> **A satisfaction survey after a resolution is ordinary. It only signals a silent

The general rule still holds where it applies (see [[classmates_com]]): a survey

## If they ignore you

Work down this list. Each rung costs them more than the one above it.

1. **Reply in the existing thread** after the statutory deadline. California
   allows 45 days for a deletion request (Cal. Civ. Code 1798.130), extendable
   once by a further 45 with notice. Quote the date you first wrote.
2. **Write to the legal entity at the registered address above**, by post, if
   email has failed. A letter to the address of record is harder to lose than a
   support ticket, and it establishes a paper trail.
3. **Complain to the California Attorney General**, who administers the data
   broker registry: <https://oag.ca.gov/contact/consumer-complaint-against-business-or-company>.
   A broker's registration is what obliges it to answer; a complaint referencing
   the registry entry is the pressure point.
4. **Complain to the FTC**: <https://reportfraud.ftc.gov>. Useful for a pattern
   of non-response rather than a single case.
5. **Your own state Attorney General.** Many states with no comprehensive
   privacy statute still have consumer-protection powers and will take a
   complaint about a business that ignores its own published policy.

**What not to bother with:** phoning a support line to argue. The person who
answers cannot change the policy and did not write it. The registry entry, the
statutory deadline and the regulator are what actually move a company.
