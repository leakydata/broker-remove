Finalsite provides websites, communications and enrolment software to schools, so on the ordinary description it holds student, parent and staff data AS A PROCESSOR ON A SCHOOL'S INSTRUCTIONS -- close to the opposite of a broker, whose defining act is selling information about people it has no relationship with. Said 'that listing is not accurate, we are a school software vendor' is a complete answer. THE SUBSTANTIVE QUESTION, asked as a question and not an allegation: IS SCHOOL DATA EVER USED BEYOND THE SCHOOL'S OWN INSTRUCTIONS -- product analytics, cross-district benchmarking, aggregate research, model training, or an enrolment/marketing product built on the aggregate. The processor boundary is where school platforms actually get into difficulty and it is invisible from outside: a school signs a contract, a parent never sees one, and the people whose data it is have no relationship with the vendor, cannot know what the contract says, and could not exercise a right without going through the school. And the population is CHILDREN -- said plainly that I am not alleging anything improper, only that it is the category where a quiet secondary use would matter most and be noticed least. THREE LIMITS: asking about ME only; NOT asking them to touch a school's records or act outside a processor mandate (name the school instead and I will approach them); and NOT asking about anybody else's record -- if a search on my identifiers surfaces someone else's data, tell me rather than act, least of all a minor's. Identifier set deliberately trimmed -- no postal history, since an education platform is unlikely to be address-keyed and I would rather not add data they do not need, with the 222 escape hatch attached. psu.edu flagged as the one most likely to appear in an education-sector system.

## Steps

*Written for anyone, not just the person who filed the original request.*

1. **Email `privacy@finalsite.com`** with a written request. Ask for four things explicitly — deletion, opt-out of sale and sharing, a direction to any third parties they sold to, and a **forward-looking suppression** so the record is not simply re-added at the next data import.
2. **List every address, email and phone number you have ever had**, not just current ones. Records are filed under whatever was current when they were created — a search on today's details misses them.
3. **Ask them to state which identifiers matched.** "We deleted your record" and "we searched and found nothing" are different outcomes, and a reply that does not distinguish them tells you nothing about whether you were ever in the file.

## If they ignore you

Work down this list. Each rung costs them more than the one above it.

1. **Reply in the existing thread** after the statutory deadline. California
   allows 45 days for a deletion request (Cal. Civ. Code 1798.130), extendable
   once by a further 45 with notice. Quote the date you first wrote.
2. **Write to the legal entity at the registered address above**, by post, if
   email has failed. A letter to the address of record is harder to lose than a
   support ticket, and it establishes a paper trail.
3. **Complain to the California Attorney General**, who administers the data
   broker registry: <https://oag.ca.gov/contact/consumer-complaint-against-business-or-company>.
   A broker's registration is what obliges it to answer; a complaint referencing
   the registry entry is the pressure point.
4. **Complain to the FTC**: <https://reportfraud.ftc.gov>. Useful for a pattern
   of non-response rather than a single case.
5. **Your own state Attorney General.** Many states with no comprehensive
   privacy statute still have consumer-protection powers and will take a
   complaint about a business that ignores its own published policy.

**What not to bother with:** phoning a support line to argue. The person who
answers cannot change the policy and did not write it. The registry entry, the
statutory deadline and the regulator are what actually move a company.
