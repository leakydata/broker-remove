All three questions answered without hedging. (1) 'Fog's Data Services opt-out and suppression process is KEYED TO THE SPECIFIC MAID SUBMITTED. Fog does not maintain a durable identity or device linkage designed to associate a subsequently reset or replaced MAID with a previously submitted MAID for opt-out purposes.' (2) 'If a Data Supplier later provides information associated with a different MAID, Fog does not have a mechanism that identifies that new MAID as the successor to a previously suppressed identifier.' (3) 'the new identifier is a different MAID and must be submitted separately ... Fog does not receive notice when a particular consumer's or device's advertising identifier is reset and therefore cannot notify the consumer when that occurs ... the suppression remains effective for the exact MAID submitted, but it does not automatically transfer to a replacement advertising identifier.' So §140 is now confirmed by the company in writing: a MAID-keyed opt-out has an expiry date set by the operating system rather than by the person who submitted it, and its lapse is silent on both sides. THE PART WORTH KEEPING is in answer 2: maintaining that relationship 'would require Fog to maintain ADDITIONAL PERSISTENT LINKAGE between identifiers, which is not part of Fog's current Data Services architecture.' A durable opt-out would require building MORE cross-identifier persistence than they keep -- the mechanism that makes the protection last is the mechanism that makes the data more invasive. Declining to build it is defensible and arguably the more privacy-protective choice. They also declined to treat this as a CCPA request because CA residency was not established, which is correct for a PA resident and consistent with §149a. Replied to record the answers and close; no further action needed from them.
