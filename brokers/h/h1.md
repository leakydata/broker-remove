## Steps

1. Email `inquiries@h1insights.com`.
2. Ask what identifiers they hold you under, and invite a written negative.
3. Ask them to search **professional** identifiers, not consumer ones.
4. Ask for the assembled profile, including inferences.
5. Ask specifically for **network edges** to other named people.

## Gotchas

A healthcare-professional profile is keyed to professional identity — employer,
title, licence or registration number, publications, a `.edu` or work address. A
consumer-shaped search of a home address will truthfully find nothing while a full
profile exists. Give them the professional keys, and ask which ones they hold you
under.

**The network edges are the part that outlives a normal deletion.** These products
map relationships — who refers to whom, who co-authors with whom, who influences
whom. An edge is personal information about **both** people, and deleting one
person's profile characteristically leaves the edge intact on the other person's
record, where it still names you.

Ask for the edges by name. It is the single ask most likely to be missed, and the
one that most affects whether the deletion means anything.

Also ask about **inferred clinical attributes** — prescribing or referral
inferences, trial involvement, influence and reach scores. Nobody supplied these;
the system derived them, which makes them personal information about the person
and not merely a fact about the industry.

Expect the processor deflection (`_DEFLECTIONS.md` §21) and ask for the customer
list in the same breath, since a profile exported into a customer's system is the
copy the deletion does not reach.

## A written negative, properly done

They replied with a formal letter — dated, addressed, signed by the company, with a
named contact:

> *"After conducting a diligent search of our records, we have not found any
> related data to delete pursuant to your request. This is likely because we do not
> process personal information about you."*

**Note the noun.** "Our records" — not "this account", not "our customer database",
not "the name and email provided". By the test in `_SILENT_FAILURES.md` §17 this is
a broad negative rather than a scoped one, which is what makes it usable as an
outcome. Recorded as `not_found`.

They also volunteered something worth having:

> *"We will maintain a record of this request for at least 24 months in order to
> meet our legal obligations and to prevent future personal information sales for
> you."*

That is a **suppression entry**, granted as a side effect of the deletion request —
the thing this project has to ask for explicitly nearly everywhere else. Retaining
the minimum data needed to honour a suppression is the correct design and worth
recognising when a company does it unprompted.

## Why this one was probably genuine

Worth saying, because the instinct after a hundred deflections is to distrust a
negative. The letter arrived roughly five hours after a request that asked
specifically about **professional** identifiers — a `.edu` address, employer, job
title, licence numbers — rather than the consumer-shaped identifiers a healthcare
data business would not hold. If they were going to find a profile, that is the
letter that would have found it.

A negative in response to a request that named the right keys is worth more than a
negative in response to a request that named the wrong ones.
