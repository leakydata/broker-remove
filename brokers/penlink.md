Penlink sells digital intelligence and investigations tooling to government and law enforcement -- their site lists digital evidence, open-source intelligence, link analysis, courtroom presentation, and 'Live Communication -- Real-time Interception'. A lawful-intercept and OSINT vendor appearing on the CONSUMER data broker register is the reason for the letter, and the reason it opens by narrowing rather than asking. THE FIRST PARAGRAPH DISCLAIMS EXPLICITLY: not asking them to delete, suppress, alter or disclose anything held for or on behalf of a law enforcement agency; anything obtained under warrant, court order, subpoena or other legal process; anything in an active or closed investigation; or anything held by customers in their own systems. Said plainly that I am not asking them to interfere with lawful investigative work and would not want them to, and that 'that category is out of scope' is an answer I will accept without argument. What IS in scope: the commercial data holdings that caused the registration, since a broker by definition sells personal information about consumers it has no relationship with. FOUR ASKS: (1) which product line or dataset is the registered one -- a company selling investigative software to agencies is not thereby a broker, the tool is not the file, so the registration implies there is also a file; (2) OSINT as standing collection vs live query, with 'a query engine that stores nothing' accepted in advance as a real answer and the Martin Data precedent cited; (3) if a standing collection exists -- match, contents, and specifically the ASSOCIATIONS GRAPH placing me as a linked person on someone else's record, which a subject-only deletion never reaches; (4) THE ACCESS LOG ASKED CAREFULLY, conceding up front that an investigative subject learning they were searched can defeat an investigation and that I am not trying to construct that -- so asking only whether any COMMERCIAL customer, outside any law-enforcement or legal-process context, has queried my identifiers, with 'we cannot distinguish the two categories' accepted as an answer. Closes with the strongest version of the common-name warning yet: in a marketing file a mismatch wastes a mailing; in an intelligence context it attaches one person's record to another's identity and the consequences fall on a stranger who will never know.
