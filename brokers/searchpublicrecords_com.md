## Gotchas

**The confirmation names a record, and the record may not be yours.** Their
opt-out confirmation reads:

> "This email is to confirm that we have removed the public record information
> for [FIRST LAST] of [TOWN], ME, age [NN] from the search results of
> SearchPublicRecords.com."

(Masked — this repo is public. What matters is that they named a specific
record: a full name, a town, a state and an age.)

The age was correct. **Bradford, Maine is not an address the subject has ever
had** — not among sixteen prior addresses across PA, MD and AL. Either they
matched on name-plus-age and suppressed a different 47-year-old with the same
name, or their source data carries a wrong locality for the subject.

Most brokers write "we have removed your information from our database", which
identifies nobody and makes this class of error undetectable. This one is
catchable only because they were unusually specific. Treat that specificity as a
gift and check it — see `_SILENT_FAILURES.md` §69 for the general rule.

**What was sent back**, and it is worth keeping the third point in any reuse:

1. Re-run the removal against the *addresses* supplied rather than a name-and-age
   match, and say how many records matched.
2. If the Bradford ME record is not the subject's, **restore it** — nobody asked
   for a stranger's listing to be taken down.
3. If it genuinely is the subject's record with a wrong locality, name the source,
   so it can be corrected upstream instead of hidden here.

Also asked, unanswered so far: is this a suppression that survives the next
ingest, or a one-time removal from the current index?

**Two senders, one company.** Replies to the marketing-platform message thread
back to a `support@searchpublicrecords.com` address, so the reply route is fine
— but note that the confirmation arriving through a bulk-mail system with an
unsubscribe footer means it is generated by a template, not typed by the agent
who worked the ticket. The agent's own Zendesk message is the vaguer of the two.

## Verification

Re-run a name search on searchpublicrecords.com against the current locality and
the two longest-held prior ones — **not** against the Maine record they named, which tells you
nothing about whether the subject is still listed. Their own notice warns that
search-engine caches take "several days to several weeks" to catch up, so a
Google result alone is not evidence either way; check on-site.
